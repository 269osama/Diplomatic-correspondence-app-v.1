Personal Note

First-person · Signed · Semi-formal
▼
First Person Signature Required No Seal Bilateral

What Is It?

A personal note is a formal diplomatic letter written in the first person — meaning you write "I" throughout. It is one of the five classic diplomatic documents and is used when a diplomat or foreign minister needs to communicate something officially but with a personal, direct voice. Think of it as the diplomat's signed letter to a counterpart.

When Is It Used?

Personal notes are used to present official positions, lodge protests, send congratulations or condolences, make notifications, or request information. They are sent between ambassadors, between foreign ministers, and between diplomatic missions and host country ministries. If a personal note is received, protocol requires it be answered with a personal note — never with a verbal note.

Format & Structure

Required Elements
  • Opening address: "Your Excellency," or "Dear Ambassador,"
  • Body: Written in first person — "I have the honour to inform you…"
  • Complimentary close: "Please accept, Excellency, the assurances of my highest consideration."
  • Signature: Signed in black ink (other colors can carry unintended meaning)
  • Name, title, date at bottom
Personal notes are NOT sealed. The signature replaces the seal as authentication. If you seal a personal note, that is an error.

Protocol Formulas

It is an honour for me to inform Your Excellency that the Government of the Republic has approved…
Please accept, Excellency, the assurances of my highest consideration.

Exception: Protest Notes

When a personal note is used as a protest note, courtesy phrases at the opening may be omitted. The tone becomes direct and the standard expression of respect may be reduced or skipped entirely.

Paper & Signing Rules

The size and quality of note paper matters in formal diplomatic practice. The signature must be in black ink — blue, red, or green ink can be interpreted differently or considered informal. No co-signers; only the official sender signs.

A personal note must be answered with a personal note. This is a firm rule of diplomatic correspondence symmetry.

Verbal Note (Note Verbale)

Third person · Sealed · No signature · Most common
▼
Third Person Sealed No Signature (usually) Most Widely Used

What Is It?

The verbal note (note verbale in French) is the single most frequently used document in diplomatic correspondence between ministries of foreign affairs and embassies. Despite its name, it is entirely written — "verbal" here refers to its origins in oral communication that was formalized. It is always written in the third person.

When Is It Used?

Verbal notes can handle almost anything: political positions, requests, protests, congratulations, economic and cultural matters, bilateral and multilateral issues. Because of their versatility, they dominate daily embassy correspondence. Like personal notes, a verbal note should be answered with a verbal note.

Format & Structure

Required Elements
  • Institutional heading: Official letterhead of the ministry or embassy
  • Registration/index number
  • Opening compliment (third person): "The Ministry of Foreign Affairs of [State] presents its compliments to…"
  • Body: Written throughout in third person — "The Ministry wishes to inform…"
  • Closing compliment: "The Ministry avails itself of this opportunity to renew to [recipient] the assurances of its highest consideration."
  • Official seal (of the ministry or embassy)
  • Date and place — written in the lower right-hand corner
  • Address of recipient — written in the lower left-hand corner
The Ministry of Foreign Affairs of the Republic of Azerbaijan presents its compliments to the Embassy of the United Kingdom and has the honour to inform...
The Embassy of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland avails itself of this opportunity to renew to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs the assurances of its highest consideration.

The Seal — History & Significance

The seal is a legal concept with ancient origins. The first known sealed treaty was between Egyptian Pharaoh Ramses II and Hittite King Hattusili III in 1278 BCE — a silver tablet authenticated by signatures and seals. In verbal notes, the seal must be placed correctly: upright, emblem facing up, writing legible. Traditionally placed at the bottom — but notably, Morocco places its seal at the top, reflecting a centuries-old diplomatic assertion of authority.

In Morocco, the seal on note verbales is placed at the TOP of the document — this is the only country that does this today, and it reflects a historical claim of precedence.

Protest Notes (as Verbal Notes)

When a verbal note expresses serious dissatisfaction — border violations, mistreatment of citizens, non-observance of international norms — the opening and closing compliments are omitted entirely. The note begins directly with the grounds for protest. This is also called Fin de non-recevoir when a note is refused upon presentation.

Multi-Page Rules

A verbal note is ideally one page. If it exceeds one page, only the first page uses official note paper with the registration number and seal. Subsequent pages are attached separately. The address appears only on the first page.

Aide-Mémoire

Meeting summary · No seal · No compliments · Hand-delivered
▼
Impersonal Style No Seal No Compliments Hand-Delivered

What Is It?

The aide-mémoire (French for "memory aid") is a brief written document summarizing key points from a diplomatic meeting or conversation. It is sometimes called a "small letter" in diplomatic sources. Its purpose is to prevent misunderstandings and provide a written record of what was discussed orally.

When Is It Used?

An ambassador hands an aide-mémoire to a foreign minister or ministry official during or after a meeting to confirm what was said. It is a quick-reaction document — useful when you need to record a position promptly without going through the full formality of a note. In recent times, aide-mémoires are becoming less common as other formats have taken over.

Format & Structure

Required Elements
  • Written on ordinary paper (not official note paper)
  • Sometimes handwritten
  • Date and location
  • No seal
  • No compliments (opening or closing)
  • Signed by the drafter (but not always)
  • Not registered with a number

Language Style

Aide-mémoires use impersonal constructions throughout — this is their stylistic signature. Instead of "I request" or "The Ministry requests," you write:

It is stated that… / It is requested that… / Attention is drawn to… / It is considered expedient that…
Aide-mémoires are NEVER sent by post or diplomatic courier. They are always delivered personally — in hand — by the ambassador or a senior diplomat during a meeting.

Limitations

An aide-mémoire is not a formal diplomatic communication in the full sense. It cannot substitute for a note on serious political matters. However, it is valuable precisely because of its informality and speed — for recording the gist of a conversation before memories fade.

Memorandum

Detailed analysis · Historical & legal context · Independent or attached
▼
Detailed Document Independent or Attached Historical & Legal Arguments Bilateral Signing Possible

What Is It?

A memorandum is a detailed diplomatic document that explains the factual, historical, and legal aspects of an issue in depth. Unlike a verbal note, which tends to be brief and practical, a memorandum lays out the full background and argumentation. It may be submitted independently or as an attachment to a verbal note.

When Is It Used?

Memorandums are used when a state needs to present a comprehensive position — legal justification, historical background, political context — on a complex issue. They are also used as Memorandums of Understanding (MoUs) in bilateral agreements, and their use in this form has revived in modern diplomacy.

Famous Historical Example

The most celebrated example in this course is the memorandum submitted by Alimardan bey Topchubashov at the Paris Peace Conference of 1918 on behalf of the Azerbaijan Democratic Republic. It presented Azerbaijan's claims for recognition, participation in the Conference, and membership in the League of Nations, backed by historical and political arguments.

Memorandums may be submitted either as independent documents OR attached to a verbal note. For serious, multi-layered issues, a state typically sends a protest verbal note + a memorandum providing the full legal and historical argument.

Modern Usage

In contemporary diplomacy, memorandums of understanding (MoUs) are signed bilaterally at the end of negotiations — like a formal handshake document. Signing procedures follow the same conventions as other multilateral and bilateral documents.

Collective Note & Identic Note

Multi-state coordination tools · Rare in modern practice
▼
Multi-State Rare Collective vs. Identic

Collective Note

A collective note is a single diplomatic note signed and sent jointly by several states to one receiving state. All signing states act together as one sender. This is used when multiple governments want to deliver a unified position or message — for example, several embassies jointly congratulating a government or expressing joint concern.

Identic Note (Identical/Parallel Note)

An identic note is the mirror image: one sending state sends notes of identical content to several states or embassies simultaneously. Each note goes to a different recipient but the text is the same. After the 2008 UN General Assembly resolution on the occupied territories of Azerbaijan, Azerbaijan sent identic notes to the ambassadors of France, Russia, and the United States — same content, different addressees.

COLLECTIVE = Several senders → One recipient. IDENTIC = One sender → Several recipients (same content).

Current Status

Both collective and identic notes are rarely used in modern diplomatic practice. They survive mainly in goodwill correspondence (joint congratulations) and in situations of coordinated diplomatic protest.

Communiqué

Joint outcome statement · Short · Post-meeting
▼
Joint Statement Short Post-Meeting 2+ States

What Is It?

A communiqué is a brief joint statement issued by two or more states, usually after a diplomatic meeting or negotiation. It summarizes what was agreed, what was discussed, or the outcome of the meeting. Think of it as the official press release of diplomacy — short, factual, jointly owned.

When Is It Used?

Communiqués are issued after summits, bilateral meetings, multilateral negotiations, or when two states agree to establish diplomatic relations. They mark diplomatic milestones formally.

A communiqué is shorter and less detailed than a memorandum. It summarizes outcomes, not arguments. If you need historical and legal depth, use a memorandum. If you need a quick joint outcome statement, use a communiqué.

Personal Letters (Semi-Official Letters)

Head-of-State level · Informal-formal · Relationship-building
▼
Head of State Level Semi-Official Hand-delivered by Ambassador High Political Importance

What Is It?

Personal letters in diplomacy — also called semi-official letters — are messages exchanged personally by heads of state, heads of government, or foreign ministers. Despite being "personal," they carry significant political weight and are part of official diplomatic activity. They express positions in a more flexible, relationship-based format than formal notes.

When Are They Used?

Personal letters are used to explain complex political positions, invite heads of state to negotiations, congratulate on elections or national holidays, condole on disasters, express gratitude, or address bilateral issues too sensitive or nuanced for formal notes. They are often published in the press, so they also serve a public diplomacy function.

Famous Examples From Your Course

French President Jacques Chirac sent a personal letter to Heydar Aliyev in 2000 about France's mediation role in the Nagorno-Karabakh conflict. U.S. President George Bush also sent a personal letter before the Key West talks. Before the Lisbon OSCE Summit in 1996, President Aliyev sent personal letters to all OSCE heads of state explaining Armenia's aggression — enabling Azerbaijan to use its veto effectively.

Personal letters between heads of state are delivered by ambassadors or heads of delegations — not through regular post. A handwritten personal letter is considered especially prestigious and creates an especially favorable impression.

Format Notes

Written on standard-size, high-quality paper. The sender's official position appears in the upper left corner. The address goes on the envelope. Content can range from formal to warm/personal depending on the relationship between heads of state. The formula "My dear friend and brother" — used by Azerbaijan's president to Türkiye's president — is an example of special closeness reflected in the opening formula.

Declaration

Official position statement · Unilateral or multilateral · Public-facing
▼
Unilateral / Bilateral / Multilateral Public Opinion Focus Official Position

What Is It?

A declaration is an official statement expressing the position of a state or group of states. It can be unilateral (one state), bilateral (two states), or multilateral (many states). Unlike notes, declarations are often aimed primarily at influencing public opinion rather than communicating directly with foreign governments.

When Is It Used?

Declarations are used to announce major policy positions, state principles, or express collective stances on international issues. They often accompany summits or major diplomatic events. Final acts of international conferences are sometimes structured as declarations.

Declarations are sometimes primarily directed at public opinion, not at specific foreign governments. This distinguishes them from notes, which always have a specific addressee.

Telegram

Rapid communication · Brief · Protocol-governed · Historical importance
▼
Brief Rapid Communication Protocol Formulas Pre-modern Dominant Form

What Is It?

Diplomatic telegrams were the primary tool of fast interstate communication before electronic systems. They follow the same protocol formulas and compositional conventions as other diplomatic documents but are shorter and more direct. Today they have been largely replaced by secure diplomatic cables and electronic communication.

Famous Historical Telegrams

  • Ems Telegram (1870) — Bismarck's edited telegram contributed to the outbreak of the Franco-Prussian War
  • Zimmermann Telegram (1917) — Germany proposed a military alliance with Mexico against the United States
  • Long Telegram (1946) — George Kennan's analysis of Soviet policy that shaped the U.S. containment strategy for the Cold War

Modern Use

Telegrams exchanged on elections, national holidays, natural disasters, or condolences remain common at the head-of-state level even today — they reflect the quality of bilateral relations. Notably, telegram exchanges are considered normal even between states with no formal diplomatic relations.

A head of state sending a telegram while flying over another state's territory is considered a gesture of great goodwill in diplomatic protocol.

Demarche

Diplomatic action · Not a document type itself · Uses all document forms
▼
Diplomatic Action Any Document Form Goal-Driven

What Is It?

A demarche is not a specific document type — it is a diplomatic action taken to achieve a specific purpose. It can involve any document in the diplomatic repertoire. The word comes from French, meaning "step" or "approach."

Rules

Even in a demarche, etiquette rules must be observed. Ultimatum-like language and threatening constructions are not permitted. The diplomat's only weapon is the word, so clarity and precision matter most. A demarche typically involves delivering a note, making a formal representation, or taking coordinated action through multiple documents simultaneously.

Remember: a demarche is a DIPLOMATIC ACTION, not a document type. It uses existing document types (personal notes, verbal notes, memorandums) as its instruments.

Statement

No specific addressee · Press-oriented · No reply required
▼
No Specific Addressee Press/Public Facing No Reply Required MFA Press Services

What Is It?

A statement is a diplomatic document that differs from notes and letters in one critical way: it is not addressed to a specific state. Instead, it is published broadly — in the press, on television, through news agencies — to communicate a state's position to the general public and international community.

Key Distinction

Unlike a verbal note (which requires a response), a statement does not obligate any specific state to respond. Even when states do respond, the original statement remains valid. Statements are considered particularly effective for influencing public opinion and revealing another state's actions to the world.

Statements do NOT require a reply. This is what distinguishes them from notes — which always require a corresponding note in return.

Press Statements

Presidential press services and MFA press centers regularly publish press statements. These are a requirement of modern reality, not an innovation — they reflect the state's position in real time and have as much impact as formal diplomatic documents, sometimes more.

Letter of Credence & Letter of Recall

Accreditation documents · Head-of-State issued
▼
Head of State → Head of State Accreditation Formal Ceremony

Letter of Credence

The Letter of Credence (also called "credentials") is the formal document issued by the Head of State of the sending country to officially confirm the ambassador's authority. It is addressed to the Head of State of the receiving country and contains: names and titles of both heads of state, information about the ambassador, a request to give credence to the ambassador's words, and a phrase expressing hope that the appointment will strengthen relations.

The original is presented to the Head of State of the receiving country. A copy goes to the Minister of Foreign Affairs. The date and time of presentation determine the ambassador's seniority in the diplomatic corps.

Exception: If the head of mission is Chargé d'Affaires (not a full ambassador), the letter is written by the Foreign Minister (not Head of State) and is addressed to the Foreign Minister of the receiving state.

Letter of Recall

The Letter of Recall is the formal document used to officially end an ambassador's mission and recall them. It is normally presented by the incoming new ambassador to the Head of State of the receiving country — completing the transition.

When Are New Credentials Needed?

  • When the rank of the diplomatic mission or head of mission changes
  • When fundamental constitutional changes occur in the sending state
  • When the Head of State changes (death of a monarch, election of a new president)

⚡ Must-Remember Rules

  • Answer a personal note with a personal note. Answer a verbal note with a verbal note.
  • Personal notes are signed in black ink only.
  • Verbal notes are written in third person and sealed — not signed (usually).
  • Aide-mémoires are always hand-delivered — never sent by post.
  • Protest notes omit compliments at opening and closing.
  • Only Morocco places the seal at the top of note verbales.
  • A demarche is an action, not a document type.
  • A statement has no specific addressee and does not require a reply.
  • Letters of Credence are issued when the Head of State changes, or when constitutional changes occur.
  • If the head of mission is Chargé d'Affaires, the letter goes from Foreign Minister to Foreign Minister, not Head of State to Head of State.
  • Collective note = many senders → one recipient. Identic note = one sender → many recipients (same text).
